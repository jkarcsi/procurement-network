# Procura — Target Group and Problem Validation Document

**Document status:** first developed version  
**Language:** English  
**Related material:** Procura one-pager / project summary  
**Project:** Procura — AI-assisted B2B RFQ and supplier network  
**First market:** Hungary  
**Initial focus:** General, non-strategic procurement needs of Hungarian SMEs with 10–100 employees  
**Recommended use:** product planning, validation interviews, MVP scope, landing page messaging, sales hypotheses

---

## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document defines who the first version of Procura is built for, what procurement problem it solves, in which situations it should be used, why customers may be willing to pay for it, and which validation steps are needed to prove real market demand.

This is not a final business plan. It is a **validation and focus document**. Its purpose is to ensure that product development, sales and market testing are not based on broad assumptions, but on a concrete target group, concrete problems and measurable hypotheses.

The first version of Procura is not a full ERP system, not a public procurement platform, not an enterprise procurement suite and not a marketplace covering every industry. The goal of the first version is:

> To help a Hungarian SME turn a short procurement need into several comparable offers quickly, structurally and traceably.

---

## 2. Strategic Starting Point

The first version of Procura is built for **general SME procurement**, but it should not start too broadly. In the long term, the product can become a horizontal B2B procurement network, but the first validation phase requires narrower and clearly defined use cases.

The initial market focus is:

**Hungarian SMEs with 10–100 employees that regularly request quotations for office, maintenance, service, equipment procurement or subcontracting tasks, but do not have a dedicated procurement department.**

This target group is a strong starting point because:

- they are already large enough to have recurring procurement needs;
- they are often still too small to have a professional procurement department;
- decision-making is faster than in large enterprises;
- their procurement processes are typically less formalized;
- the owner, managing director or operations manager directly feels the time loss;
- many of their purchases are not strategic, but are still financially and operationally important;
- manual RFQ writing, emailing and comparison are a real burden for them.

## 3. Primary Target Group

### 3.1 Company Size

The primary target group:

- **Hungarian SMEs with 10–100 employees**;
- typically purchase from several external service providers or suppliers during the year;
- do not have a dedicated procurement team;
- procurement decisions are often handled by the managing director, office manager, finance/admin employee, operations manager or site manager.

### 3.2 Typical Company Profile

Companies in Procura’s first target group typically:

- operate in services, commerce, light industry, manufacturing, logistics or office-based businesses;
- use several external service providers;
- are not large enough to use procurement systems such as SAP Ariba or Coupa;
- do not want a complex ERP implementation;
- are willing to test simple, fast and practical solutions;
- value tools that provide concrete time savings and better comparability.

### 3.3 Industries Where the Problem May Be Especially Relevant

The initial focus does not need to be limited to one vertical industry, but the problem is likely to be stronger in the following sectors:

- office-based service companies;
- smaller manufacturing companies;
- logistics and warehousing companies;
- property management and facility-related companies;
- hospitality and HoReCa businesses;
- private healthcare providers and medical offices;
- education, training and children’s activity businesses;
- webshops and e-commerce companies;
- construction, installation and contractor SMEs;
- smaller units of franchise networks;
- small businesses operating multiple sites.

## 4. Secondary Target Group: Supplier Side

Procura is a two-sided business system. The buyer side only receives value if there are enough relevant suppliers willing to respond to RFQs.

The secondary target group is:

**local and national service-provider SMEs, subcontractors, smaller trade companies and B2B service providers that want to acquire new business customers.**

### 4.1 Typical Supplier Profiles

- cleaning companies;
- air conditioning / HVAC providers;
- security service companies;
- occupational safety and fire safety providers;
- IT support companies;
- office supply and equipment suppliers;
- printing service providers;
- courier and logistics partners;
- maintenance providers;
- electricians;
- plumbing, gas and heating technicians;
- landscaping and site maintenance companies;
- construction subcontractors;
- marketing agencies;
- accountants, payroll and HR service providers;
- legal, compliance or consulting service providers.

### 4.2 Supplier Motivation

For suppliers, the value of Procura is not that they can “register on yet another platform,” but that they receive relevant, decision-ready business opportunities.

They will return if:

- the RFQs are real;
- the inquiries are not irrelevant;
- responding is fast;
- they do not need to build a complex profile before submitting their first offer;
- they see that responding is worth their time;
- the system does not feel like spam or advertising, but like a source of concrete business opportunities.

## 5. Typical Procurement Situations

The first version of Procura is mainly suitable for situations where the buyer does not need a large enterprise tendering process, but simple Google searches and manual emailing are already not enough.

### 5.1 Office Operations-Related Procurement

Examples:

- office supplies;
- printers, laptops, monitors or other office equipment;
- printing, copying or document management services;
- water dispensers, coffee machines and kitchen supplies;
- office furniture;
- office relocation;
- office cleaning;
- receptionist or administrative services.

Typical problem:

The company wants to see multiple offers quickly, but does not want to write separately to 10 suppliers and does not want to build a comparison table manually in Excel.

### 5.2 Maintenance and Facility-Related Procurement

Examples:

- air conditioning maintenance;
- electrical work;
- plumbing;
- heating and building systems maintenance;
- warehouse or site maintenance;
- camera or access control systems;
- fire safety;
- occupational safety;
- pest control;
- landscaping;
- snow removal.

Typical problem:

Supplier selection often happens through personal connections, with little comparable data. Management does not know whether the price is realistic and has difficulty finding new alternatives.

### 5.3 Service Procurement

Examples:

- accounting;
- payroll;
- legal services;
- marketing;
- website development;
- IT operations;
- system administration;
- HR services;
- training;
- insurance or financial consulting.

Typical problem:

Service scopes are difficult to compare. One supplier provides only a price, another provides a detailed package, a third provides an hourly rate and a fourth provides a monthly fee. The buyer has difficulty understanding what is actually included in the price.

### 5.4 Equipment Procurement

Examples:

- office and IT equipment;
- warehouse equipment;
- shelving systems;
- packaging materials;
- smaller machines;
- workwear;
- security technology equipment;
- consumables;
- special industrial supplies.

Typical problem:

Prices, delivery deadlines, warranties and additional services are difficult to compare. The person responsible for procurement often asks only a few familiar sources.

### 5.5 Subcontracting Tasks

Examples:

- construction sub-tasks;
- trade-specific work;
- warehouse or logistics support;
- event organization sub-tasks;
- installation or assembly work;
- temporary capacity expansion.

Typical problem:

The buyer wants to find several reliable partners quickly, while availability, capacity, regional coverage and price all matter at the same time.

## 6. Current Solutions

Today, the target group typically does not use a professional procurement system. Instead, they rely on simple and fragmented methods.

### 6.1 Recommendations from Acquaintances

Advantages:

- fast;
- trust-based;
- requires little search effort.

Disadvantages:

- narrow supplier pool;
- not necessarily cost-effective;
- not documented;
- difficult to compare;
- often keeps the same suppliers in place for years.

### 6.2 Google Search

Advantages:

- easy to access;
- provides many results;
- useful for initial research.

Disadvantages:

- many irrelevant results;
- quality is hard to verify;
- requires a lot of manual work;
- no offer comparison;
- no process tracking;
- strong SEO does not necessarily mean the supplier is the best choice.

### 6.3 Email and Phone

Advantages:

- everyone knows how to use them;
- no new system is needed;
- flexible.

Disadvantages:

- time-consuming;
- information can easily get lost;
- offers are not standardized;
- difficult to search later;
- no status tracking;
- no automatic reminders;
- no learning database.

### 6.4 Excel / Google Sheets

Advantages:

- simple;
- flexible;
- inexpensive.

Disadvantages:

- manual data entry;
- prone to errors;
- no automated comparison;
- no supplier-side workflow;
- no integrated communication;
- does not scale well.

### 6.5 Price Comparison and Directory Sites

Advantages:

- useful in certain categories;
- can show many providers.

Disadvantages:

- often B2C-focused;
- do not provide a structured B2B RFQ workflow;
- work less well for services;
- no needs assessment and decision workflow;
- no real procurement log.

### 6.6 Enterprise Procurement Systems

Advantages:

- professional;
- capable of complex workflows;
- can work well in large organizations.

Disadvantages:

- expensive;
- too complex for a 10–100-person SME;
- implementation takes a long time;
- many functions are unnecessary;
- not optimized for everyday procurement problems of Hungarian SMEs.

## 7. Pain Points

### 7.1 Time Loss

The buyer spends a lot of time on:

- describing the need;
- finding suppliers;
- writing emails;
- making phone calls;
- following up;
- collecting offers;
- standardizing offers;
- creating decision criteria.

Procura’s value here: speeding up intake, structuring, shortlisting, follow-up and comparison.

### 7.2 Poorly Written RFQs

Many RFQs are too vague. As a result:

- suppliers interpret them differently;
- incomplete offers arrive;
- many clarification questions are needed;
- the buyer receives non-comparable responses;
- the offer process slows down.

Procura’s value here: clarification questions, category-specific templates and structured RFQs.

### 7.3 Non-Comparable Offers

Incoming offers may differ in:

- pricing logic;
- service scope;
- warranty;
- delivery deadline;
- extra fees;
- payment terms;
- commitments.

Procura’s value here: a more standardized offer structure and comparison view.

### 7.4 Too Few Relevant Suppliers

Buyers often ask only a few known companies. This:

- reduces competition;
- may lead to higher prices;
- increases the chance of choosing suppliers out of habit;
- makes it harder to find new and better partners.

Procura’s value here: category- and region-based supplier matching, and later lead discovery.

### 7.5 Lack of Trust

The buyer does not know:

- how reliable the supplier is;
- whether they respond on time;
- whether they actually serve the given region;
- whether other companies were satisfied with them;
- whether their price is realistic.

The supplier does not know:

- whether the RFQ is real;
- whether it is worth spending time on it;
- how serious the buyer is;
- whether a decision will actually be made at the end of the process.

Procura’s value here: profiles, response statistics, ratings, audit trail and later reputation systems.

### 7.6 No Decision Trail

SMEs often do not keep structured documentation about:

- who received the RFQ;
- who responded;
- under what conditions;
- why a given supplier won;
- when the process should be repeated;
- what historical prices were.

Procura’s value here: statuses, event log, decision recording and searchable history.

## 8. Why Would They Pay?

Procura becomes a paid product if it provides not only convenience, but measurable business value.

### 8.1 Time Savings

The time of a managing director, operations manager or office manager is expensive. If an RFQ process is reduced from 2–6 hours to 20–40 minutes, that alone can justify a smaller monthly fee.

Payment logic:

> If Procura saves only a few hours of management or administrative time per month, a smaller monthly subscription can already be justified.

### 8.2 Better Price or Better Terms

If the system brings in more relevant suppliers, competition may improve. The buyer does not always need to choose the cheapest offer, but it is already valuable to see the market range.

Payment logic:

> If a single procurement process improves price or terms by just a few percent, it may cover several months of subscription fees.

### 8.3 Fewer Bad Decisions

A poorly chosen service provider can cost more in the long term than the RFQ process itself. Procura does not guarantee a perfect decision, but it can provide a better information base.

Payment logic:

> Better comparison reduces the chance that the company contracts with an expensive supplier, poor conditions or an unsuitable provider.

### 8.4 Transparency and Control

For company leadership, it is valuable to see:

- what procurement processes are running;
- who responded;
- what decision was made;
- what historical performance a supplier has shown;
- when it is worth requesting new offers.

Payment logic:

> Procura is not only an RFQ tool. Over time, it can become a lightweight procurement record system.

### 8.5 Supplier Side: New Customer Acquisition

Suppliers may pay if Procura brings them real, relevant RFQs.

Payment logic:

> In many supplier categories, a single acquired business customer may be worth much more than a monthly premium profile or qualified lead fee.

## 9. Purchasing Decision-Makers

### 9.1 Typical Buyer-Side Decision-Makers

- managing director;
- owner;
- operations manager;
- finance manager;
- office manager;
- site manager;
- employee responsible for procurement tasks.

### 9.2 Typical Users

- administrative employee;
- office manager;
- assistant;
- operations employee;
- finance/admin colleague;
- in smaller organizations, the managing director directly.

### 9.3 Typical Payer

- the company;
- a company subscription approved by the managing director;
- for smaller packages, a card-based SaaS subscription;
- for larger packages, invoiced quarterly or annual contracts.

### 9.4 Buying Process for Procura

In the early stage, the buying process is likely to be practical and relatively fast, not a long enterprise sales cycle:

1. the company leader or operations manager recognizes the problem;
2. they test one RFQ process;
3. they see whether relevant offers arrive;
4. if it works, a smaller monthly fee becomes acceptable;
5. later they may upgrade to a multi-user or higher-tier package.

## 10. Possible Pricing and Average Monthly Fee

Pricing must be validated, but as an initial hypothesis, Procura can be positioned as a small-to-mid-sized SaaS fee on the buyer side.

### 10.1 Buyer-Side Package Hypothesis

| Package | Purpose | Monthly fee hypothesis | Content |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Free / Trial | first trial | HUF 0 | 1 RFQ/month, limited features |
| Starter | smaller SME | HUF 9,900–19,900/month | a few RFQs/month, basic comparison |
| Pro | more active SME | HUF 29,900–49,900/month | more RFQs, team features, better analysis, export |
| Team | multiple users / multiple sites | HUF 69,900–99,900/month | multiple users, more categories, advanced reports |
| Enterprise / Managed | custom needs | custom fee | concierge sourcing, integrations, multiple sites |

### 10.2 Supplier-Side Package Hypothesis

| Package | Purpose | Monthly fee hypothesis | Content |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Supplier Free | supply-side building | HUF 0 | profile, limited offer submission |
| Supplier Plus | active supplier | HUF 9,900–19,900/month | more offers, better profile, notifications |
| Supplier Premium | growth-oriented service provider | HUF 29,900–49,900/month | highlighted profile, more categories/regions, more leads |
| Qualified Lead | later model | HUF 1,000–10,000/lead | only after relevant, qualified opportunity |
| Success Fee | later model | based on deal value | only after successful deal |

### 10.3 Realistic Average Monthly Fee in the Early Stage

In the first paid phase, it is more realistic to assume a lower entry ARPA:

- buyer side: **HUF 15,000–40,000/month/company**;
- supplier side: **HUF 10,000–30,000/month/supplier**;
- more active, multi-user companies: **HUF 50,000–100,000/month**;
- concierge / managed pilot: potentially **HUF 100,000–300,000/month** as a project or service fee.

Important: in the MVP phase, the goal is not to maximize price immediately. The goal is to discover:

- which side is willing to pay first;
- what value they pay for;
- whether subscription, credits or lead fees are easier to accept;
- which category has the strongest return on investment.

## 11. Beachhead Market

In the long term, Procura may become a general SME procurement platform, but the first market entry requires a focused beachhead market.

### 11.1 Recommended Beachhead Definition

**Recurring, local or regional B2B service and facility-related procurement needs of Hungarian SMEs with 10–100 employees.**

This is consistent with the general procurement positioning, but narrow enough to build the first supplier-side liquidity.

### 11.2 Initial Categories

Recommended initial categories:

1. cleaning services;
2. air conditioning / HVAC;
3. security services;
4. occupational safety;
5. fire safety;
6. IT support.

### 11.3 Second-Wave Categories

In the next wave, it may be worth adding:

- office supplies and office equipment;
- printing services;
- office furniture;
- courier and logistics services;
- warehouse equipment;
- maintenance trade work;
- landscaping / site maintenance;
- pest control;
- accounting;
- HR and payroll;
- marketing and web services;
- smaller construction or trade subcontracting tasks.

### 11.4 Why Not Every Category at Once?

In two-sided marketplaces, liquidity is more important than the number of categories. If Procura starts with too many categories, each category will have too few suppliers and too little buyer activity.

The goal is not to solve every procurement problem in the first month. The goal is to prove in a few categories that:

- buyer demand exists;
- the supplier side responds;
- the process is faster than the manual method;
- offers are more comparable;
- someone is willing to pay.

## 12. Validation Hypotheses

### 12.1 Buyer Hypotheses

1. Hungarian SMEs with 10–100 employees frequently request quotations for external services or equipment.
2. Their current RFQ process is manual, time-consuming and difficult to search later.
3. Buyers value a system that turns a short need into a structured RFQ.
4. Buyers value access to more relevant suppliers.
5. Buyers value seeing offers in a comparable format.
6. Buyers are willing to pay if the system saves time or results in better decisions.
7. Buyers do not necessarily want to buy “AI”; they want a faster, better and more transparent procurement process.

### 12.2 Supplier Hypotheses

1. Smaller service providers want to acquire new business customers.
2. A relevant and concrete RFQ is more valuable to them than a generic advertising placement.
3. Suppliers are willing to register if they can respond to a real RFQ.
4. Suppliers are willing to pay if the platform regularly brings relevant opportunities.
5. Suppliers accept a more standardized offer structure if it is not too complicated.
6. It is important for suppliers not to have to provide too much data before their first response.

### 12.3 Marketplace Hypotheses

1. On average, at least 2–3 relevant offers can be obtained for one RFQ.
2. Initial supply gaps can be bridged with concierge or lead discovery methods.
3. A buyers-first launch can work if the system can involve external suppliers.
4. Supplier response rates improve if the inquiry is concrete, category-specific and easy to answer.
5. Category- and region-based focus builds liquidity faster than a national, all-category launch.

## 13. Validation Plan

### 13.1 First 30 Days: Problem Validation

Goal:

- understand whether the target group actually buys this way;
- identify the strongest pain points;
- find the first categories where RFQs are frequent and painful.

Tasks:

- 20–30 buyer interviews;
- 20–30 supplier interviews;
- detailed reconstruction of 5–10 past procurement processes;
- select 3–5 categories for validation;
- test landing page messages;
- build the first version of the supplier database.

### 13.2 Second 30 Days: Concierge Pilot

Goal:

- prove that the process works commercially, without software automation or with only partial manual automation.

Tasks:

- run 5–10 real RFQs;
- manually or semi-automatically refine RFQs;
- manually invite suppliers;
- collect offers in a structured way;
- prepare manual offer comparison views;
- collect buyer feedback;
- collect supplier feedback.

### 13.3 Third 30 Days: MVP Validation

Goal:

- test the first software-based workflow with real companies.

Tasks:

- buyer intake;
- clarification questions;
- structured RFQ;
- supplier shortlist;
- sending the RFQ;
- supplier response;
- offer comparison;
- decision recording;
- willingness-to-pay testing.

## 14. Buyer Interview Questions

### 14.1 Basic Operations

1. How often do you request quotations from external service providers or suppliers?
2. In which categories does this happen most frequently?
3. Who usually writes the RFQ?
4. How many suppliers do you usually contact?
5. How long does an average RFQ process take?
6. Where do you store incoming offers?
7. How do you compare offers?

### 14.2 Pain Points

1. What is the most frustrating part of requesting offers?
2. Where does the process usually get stuck?
3. What happens if too few offers arrive?
4. Have you ever made a poor supplier decision? What caused it?
5. How easy is it to find new suppliers?
6. Is it difficult to describe exactly what you need?
7. Do you often receive offers that are difficult to compare?

### 14.3 Willingness to Pay

1. Would you pay for a system that helps create RFQs faster?
2. Would you pay for receiving more relevant offers?
3. Would you pay to see offers in a comparable format?
4. Would you prefer a monthly fee, a one-time fee or a success fee?
5. What monthly fee would feel like a “no-brainer”?
6. At what price would management approval be required?

## 15. Supplier Interview Questions

### 15.1 Customer Acquisition

1. Where do new business customers come from today?
2. How difficult is it to acquire new B2B customers?
3. Do you use any lead generation or RFQ platforms?
4. What kind of RFQs are you happy to respond to?

3. In what format do you prefer to submit offers?
4. Is it important to be able to ask questions before submitting an offer?

### 15.3 Willingness to Pay

1. Would you pay for relevant and concrete B2B RFQs?
2. Would you prefer a monthly fee, a lead fee or a success fee?
3. What would a well-matched RFQ be worth to you?
4. When would you feel that the platform pays for itself?
5. What guarantee would you expect before paying?

## 16. Success Metrics

### 16.1 Problem Validation Metrics

- at least 60% of interviewed buyers regularly request quotations;
- at least 50% use manual and fragmented methods;
- at least 40% say offer comparison is problematic;
- at least 30% indicate some willingness to pay;
- recurring pain points appear in at least 3 categories.

### 16.2 Concierge Pilot Metrics

- at least 5 real RFQs are completed;
- at least 5 relevant suppliers can be contacted per RFQ;
- at least 2 relevant offers arrive on average per RFQ;
- at least half of buyers say the process is better than their previous method;
- at least 1–2 buyers would be willing to pay next time.

### 16.3 MVP Metrics

- buyer onboarding completion rate;
- RFQ creation rate;
- RFQ publishing rate;
- supplier response rate;
- average number of offers per RFQ;
- RFQ closing rate;
- number of accepted offers;
- buyer return rate;
- supplier return rate;
- payment conversion.

## 17. Go / No-Go Decision Points

### 17.1 Go Signals

It is worth continuing development if:

- buyers spontaneously understand the problem;
- RFQ needs are recurring in the target group;
- suppliers respond to real RFQs;
- supply can be built quickly in at least a few categories;
- the buyer side shows clear time savings;
- the supplier side shows customer acquisition value;
- at least one side is willing to pay.

### 17.2 No-Go or Pivot Signals

Be cautious if:

- buyers rarely request quotations;
- the problem is not painful enough;
- suppliers do not respond;
- one RFQ does not receive at least 2 relevant offers;
- the process requires too much manual work;
- nobody wants to pay;
- marketplace cold start is too expensive;
- buyers prefer a fully manual concierge service instead of software.

---

## 18. Product Planning Consequences

Based on the target group, the following are most important in the MVP:

1. **Very fast RFQ creation.** The buyer should not feel administrative burden.
2. **Clarification questions.** The system should help formulate the need better.
3. **Category- and region-based matching.** The shortlist should be understandable and explainable.
4. **Simple supplier response.** Suppliers should not get stuck on registration or complex interfaces.
5. **Offer comparison.** This should be one of the main buyer-side value points.
6. **Audit trail.** Important business events should be searchable later.
7. **Admin tools.** Early supply gaps must be actively managed.
8. **Category expansion requests.** If there is no suitable category, it should not become a dead end.
9. **Simple Hungarian-language communication.** Messaging should focus on business outcomes, not technology.
10. **Do not position around AI alone.** Buyers do not buy “AI”; they buy a faster and better RFQ process.

## 20. Summary

Procura’s first target group is not every business and not every procurement situation. The best initial focus is:

> Hungarian SMEs with 10–100 employees that regularly request quotations for office, maintenance, service, equipment procurement or subcontracting tasks, but do not have a dedicated procurement department.

The strongest initial problem is not that these companies cannot buy. The problem is that their procurement process is:

- manual;
- slow;
- fragmented;
- difficult to compare;
- poorly documented;
- dependent on too few relevant suppliers.

The first version of Procura must prove that a simpler and more structured RFQ process can:

- save time;
- create better RFQs;
- involve more relevant suppliers;
- produce more comparable offers;
- support documented decisions;
- and generate willingness to pay on at least one side of the marketplace.

If this can be proven in a few narrow categories, Procura can later expand into a general SME procurement marketplace and supplier network.
